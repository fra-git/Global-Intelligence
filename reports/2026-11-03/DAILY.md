# Europe & Italy

## Italy

Italy's main domestic story today is the sale of the ex Ilva steelworks. Banks and energy prices are the main economic stories.

### Ex Ilva: Italian steel consortium may drop out days before the bid deadline
Press reports say the Federacciai consortium of Italian steelmakers could withdraw its bid for the cold-rolling part of ex Ilva. Federacciai is the steel industry association. Its president, Antonio Gozzi, said "the decision has not yet been taken" and that it will be announced once made. Binding offers are due on 8 November. A meeting with industrialists took place at Palazzo Chigi on 2 November, and ANSA reports no government position.

**Why it matters:** ex Ilva, now Acciaierie d'Italia and under extraordinary administration, is Italy's largest steel complex, based in Taranto. In August Federacciai filed an expression of interest for the plants, excluding the hot area of Taranto. Reports at the time named 14 companies and put the group's valuation at €600m-€1bn. The Jindal group, an Indian steel producer, was reported to have offered about €2bn for the whole site. If the Italian group pulls out, Jindal's offer becomes the main alternative.

**Watch:** Federacciai's decision and the 8 November offers.

*Sources: [ANSA](https://www.ansa.it/sito/notizie/topnews/2026/11/03/gozzi-decisione-non-ancora-presa-su-ex-ilva-quando-lo-sara-la-comunicheremo_18cfe301-fb5e-4204-bd34-fa8997173ebc.html), [Il Foglio](https://www.ilfoglio.it/en/economy/2026/08/19/news/federacciai-submits-an-expression-of-interest-regarding-the-former-ilva-plants--405127)*

## China

Beijing is trying to cool a currency rally while a stock-support push has yet to convince investors. Trump's trip to Shenzhen in about two weeks frames its diplomacy.

### PBOC moves to cool the yuan
The People's Bank of China (PBOC) asked banks to hold more foreign currency in reserve. The onshore yuan then ended its longest winning streak in three decades, according to Bloomberg. A separate Bloomberg newsletter says Beijing's effort to lift stocks "falls flat".

**Why it matters:** A strong yuan hurts exporters, who were already weak in October factory data. Authorities want to slow the gains without undermining confidence. Raising the reserve requirement makes it costlier for banks to supply foreign currency, which reduces demand for yuan.

**Market impact:** The onshore yuan weakened on the day. Chinese equities did not respond as officials hoped.

**Watch:** Further PBOC steps and stimulus signals before the Shenzhen summit.

## Explained

### BTP-Bund spread
It is the gap between the yield on Italy's 10-year bond (BTP) and Germany's (Bund). Investors treat Germany as the safe benchmark, so the spread measures the extra return they demand to hold Italian debt. Think of it as the premium on insurance: a riskier borrower pays more. Today the BTP yields 4.47% and the Bund 3.31%, a gap of 115.5 basis points (one basis point is 0.01 percentage points). It was about 113 on Monday. It matters because it drives Italy's borrowing costs and reflects worries about the budget and the ECB's direction. Key number: 115.5.

### ECB presidency and the nomination process
The ECB president chairs the Governing Council, which sets euro-area interest rates. Eurozone governments nominate candidates, finance ministers in the Eurogroup discuss them, and leaders and the European Parliament follow. In practice, Berlin and Paris must both agree. It is like choosing a referee who both big teams accept. The pick matters now because inflation is above target and the Council is split on hikes. Key date: 6 November.

### Strait of Hormuz and the oil and gas price
Hormuz is the narrow sea passage between Iran and Oman through which a large share of the world's oil and liquefied natural gas (LNG) moves. If it is closed, supply shrinks, and prices rise. Think of a motorway bottleneck. Markets then trade on every headline about talks, which explains Monday's 7.56% fall and today's rebound. Higher gas prices reach Italian power bills. Key number: Brent around $104-106.

### Reserve requirements and currency control
A central bank can ask banks to hold more of a currency in reserve. That takes some of it out of circulation and raises its price. China's PBOC asked banks to hold more foreign currency, which makes it costlier for banks to supply dollars and so slows the yuan's rise, because a strong yuan hurts exporters. It works like a thermostat on the exchange rate. Key fact: the yuan's winning streak was its longest in three decades.

## Glossary

- **Basis point:** one hundredth of a percentage point; 100 basis points equal 1%.
- **Brent / WTI:** the main European and US oil price benchmarks.
- **TTF:** the Dutch gas futures contract that is Europe's benchmark gas price.
- **Eurogroup:** the monthly meeting of eurozone finance ministers.
- **ECB:** the European Central Bank, which sets eurozone interest rates.
- **Federacciai:** the Italian steel industry federation, led by Antonio Gozzi.
- **Acciaierie d'Italia (ex Ilva):** Italy's largest steel complex, in Taranto, under extraordinary administration.
- **PAC-3:** Lockheed Martin's Patriot missile interceptor.
- **PBOC:** China's central bank.
- **AGOA:** the US law that gives eligible African countries duty-free access to US markets.
- **ACN:** Italy's national cybersecurity agency.
- **Midterms:** US elections held midway through a presidential term.
