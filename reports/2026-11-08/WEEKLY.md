# Europe & Italy

## Italy

**Overall picture:** The week was dominated by a cyber incident that reached the foreign minister himself. Alongside it, Italy kept its place in a fragile market and energy backdrop. The BTP-Bund spread, which measures the extra yield Italy pays over Germany, has eased from its early-October peak but remains well above September levels. The 2027 budget bill is moving through parliament. The feed gave little day-by-day detail on the parliamentary stages.

### Pro-Russian hackers leak foreign minister Tajani's personal data
On 7 November Foreign Minister Antonio Tajani said hackers had published his personal data online. Prime Minister Giorgia Meloni blamed pro-Russian hackers, according to Bloomberg and the BBC. On 8 November Interior Minister Matteo Piantedosi called the national public-security committee to the Viminale (the Interior Ministry) for Tuesday 10 November to assess the cyber threat.

**Why it matters:** This is the latest in a run of hybrid attacks (cyber, disinformation, sabotage) on Italy. In February 2026 Tajani said the security services had thwarted Russian-linked attacks on Foreign Ministry sites and on the Milano-Cortina Olympics. The pro-Russian group Noname057(16) claimed some of those attacks. Across Europe this week, scores of flights received bomb hoaxes, and a cyberattack hit the UK meat industry and affected supermarket freezers in several countries. European agencies are probing whether the hoaxes began in Russia. A Russian extremist group has denied sending them.

**For Europe & Italy:** Rome is treating this as a national-security issue, not just IT. Expect pressure to strengthen the National Cybersecurity Agency (ACN).

**Watch:** The outcome of Tuesday's Viminale meeting, and whether Italy formally attributes the attack.

### Spread near 113 points as ECB warns inflation will stay high
The BTP-Bund spread closed at 113.83 basis points on 3 November, after about 113 the day before. In September it had fallen to roughly 88-89. It then rose above 100 on 30 September and, per ItaliaOggi, touched 131 on 1 October. On 7 November ECB President Christine Lagarde told Japan's Yomiuri Shimbun that inflation will likely stay high in the near term, with second-round effects (wage and price knock-ons) no worse than expected. On 6 November global equities fell on fears of a US AI slowdown, a risk-off move that usually widens spreads.

**Market impact:** The 10-year BTP yield is around 4.4%. A higher inflation path for longer limits the room for ECB rate cuts. That weighs on Italian debt-servicing costs and on bank margins in different directions.

**Watch:** The Commission's autumn forecast, traditionally in mid-November, and the final budget votes before year-end.

## European Union

**Overall picture:** The week was about who will lead the ECB after Christine Lagarde, and about Europe's anxiety over a possible US-Russia security deal. Regulation and defence files also moved.

### Two candidates to succeed Lagarde at the ECB
The Eurogroup (euro-area finance ministers) closed nominations on Friday 6 November with two names. They are Spain's Pablo Hernández de Cos, who now heads the Bank for International Settlements, and the Netherlands' Klaas Knot, a former Dutch central bank chief. Ministers hold a first review on Monday 9 November. The European Council makes the final appointment. One report says the new president would take office in February. Analysts see Knot as slightly more hawkish, meaning more focused on fighting inflation, and Hernández de Cos as slightly more dovish. They say the differences are modest. A Knot win would require his fellow Dutchman Frank Elderson to leave the ECB board, because there should not be two board members from one country. Japan Times notes a surprise entrant could still appear, as Lagarde did in 2019.

**Market impact:** Lagarde said on 7 November that inflation will stay high for now. The choice of successor shapes rate expectations for the euro.

**Watch:** The 9 November Eurogroup, then the leaders' summit.

## Italy in the EU & PNRR

**Overall picture:** I found no Council or Parliament vote this week in which Italy's position stood out. Italy's exposure was in three places: the ECB succession, a tobacco tax proposal and its fiscal standing.

### Tobacco excise: tougher EU rules, more time for Italy
The Irish presidency of the EU Council proposed a new minimum excise duty on tobacco. It would start at 10% of price or €30 per kilo and rise gradually to 50% or €50 per kilo from 2032. It would also tighten rules on purchases from abroad. Il Sole 24 Ore reports Italy would get a longer transition. Tax files need unanimity, so Rome holds a veto and its tobacco industry has leverage.

**Watch:** The next ECOFIN (finance ministers') discussion of the text.

*Source: [Il Sole 24 Ore](https://www.ilsole24ore.com/art/tabacco-stretta-ue-acquisti-esteri-e-nuove-accise-ma-piu-tempo-italia-AJCjyb8B)*

### Fiscal standing and the ECB race
In May the Commission forecast an Italian 2026 deficit of 2.9% of GDP. That is below the 3% ceiling and would let Italy leave the excessive deficit procedure, the EU's corrective track for overspenders. The exit would normally be decided after final 2026 data in spring 2027. The autumn forecast and the Commission's budget opinions are the next checkpoint. Italy has no candidate for the ECB presidency, which is a contest between Spain and the Netherlands.

# Great Powers

## United States

Democrats won control of Congress in Tuesday's midterms. The Associated Press called the House for them early Wednesday, and early reports showed them flipping several Senate seats, with Mississippi and Alaska still uncalled at that point. The new Congress convenes on 3 January. Trump gave mixed messages: he congratulated Democrats while distancing himself from Republican losses.

### Trump doubles down: Miller becomes chief of staff
Within two days, Trump replaced Susie Wiles with Stephen Miller, the architect of his immigration policy. Miller takes over on Monday, according to NBC News. Some House Republicans reportedly objected, and Democratic leader Hakeem Jeffries called him a "reckless extremist." On Saturday Trump told supporters, per ANSA, that he will use his veto power and that the MAGA movement will not quit if results disappoint.

**Why it matters:** Miller's promotion signals confrontation, not accommodation. Bloomberg's analysts note Trump keeps wide unilateral authority over tariffs and executive action. Democrats cannot override a veto without two-thirds majorities. Their leverage is oversight, subpoenas, spending bills and nominations.

**Watch:** The first investigations in January. The Financial Times reports crypto firms bracing for probes into the Trump family's business ties.

> **Background — tariffs and the courts:** The Supreme Court struck down most of Trump's emergency tariffs earlier this year, according to press reports. Trump has since leaned on other legal authorities, and Miller publicly attacked the justices.

### Markets: AI sell-off and a fuel squeeze
Global stocks fell on Friday as fears of tougher scrutiny of data-centre construction and a US AI slowdown spread to Asia and Europe (BBC). Bloomberg describes the AI trade as unravelling after the vote, pushing investors toward Chinese and Indian equities. Separately, the FT reported on 4 November that Trump's diesel arrangement with Russia is faltering. Two tankers changed course while US fuel stocks sit at a 20-year low.

**Market impact:** US tech and AI-linked shares are the epicentre of the fall. Fuel prices are politically sensitive for the White House. I could not verify this week's Fed or jobs figures, so I leave them out. Check the Fed's next meeting, due in December, and the October inflation report, due in mid-November.

### Foreign policy: Russia, China and Congress
Washington is pushing Russia–Ukraine talks in Abu Dhabi (see Russia & Ukraine). It is also pressing Beijing to honour the Busan trade deal (see China). The FT reports that European officials fear a US–Russia security deal that would undermine NATO and restore a Russian sphere of influence. Bloomberg says a Democratic Congress could constrain Trump through spending decisions, Ukraine aid, Russia sanctions and oversight.

## Deep Dive

### The energy shock that turned into a rate cycle

**Background.** Before the Iran war, central banks were close to done with tightening. The war began about nine months ago and closed the Strait of Hormuz, through which a large share of the world's oil and LNG (liquefied natural gas) passes. Brent rose from about $72 to over $115. Dutch TTF gas, Europe's benchmark, topped €100/MWh in late October, a four-year high. EU storage was only 74.6% full on 25 October against a five-year average near 86%.

**The actors.** The ECB must control inflation without crushing economies that are already weak. It hiked in June and September and held the deposit rate at 2.50% on 29 October. Governing Council hawks warn markets may underestimate the tightening needed. Lagarde says inflation will stay high near term and second-round effects (energy feeding wages and other prices) are no worse than expected. The Bank of England has hiked, and the Fed's two dissenters wanted a hike. A strong US jobs report (165,000 against about 65,000 expected) raises the stakes.

**What changed this week.** October inflation rose to 4.0%, with Italy at 5.6%, France 3.8%, Spain 4.9% and Germany 2.8% (helped by a temporary fuel-tax cut). Core inflation is 2.5%, so the pressure is still mostly energy. Then oil fell 16% in a week. That drop rests on reports of diplomacy, not on any reopening. Washington, Tehran and the IRGC (Iran's Revolutionary Guards) claimed more mine attacks on tankers, and Iran's leadership rejects softening. Eurasia Group reportedly sees a deal at 10%.

The ECB also faces a leadership change. Lagarde leaves on 7 February 2027. Knot is seen as slightly more hawkish and Hernández de Cos as slightly more dovish, with modest differences. A Knot win would require Dutchman Frank Elderson to leave the ECB board. A late surprise entrant is still possible, as Lagarde herself was in 2019.

**Italy and Europe.** Italy is the most exposed large economy. Inflation is above the euro-area average, wages lag, and Q3 GDP was flat. The Commission's spring forecast put 2026 growth at 0.5%. The 2027 budget of about €26bn leans on EU approval of a €29bn deviation across 2027-28, and about 85% of the extra €10.9bn for defence and security falls in those years. A December hike would add to debt-service costs and to the cost of floating-rate mortgages. Yet the spread has held near 113 basis points, well below 131 on 1 October. Strong bank earnings, such as Intesa's, help.

**How it could evolve.** There are three paths.

- **Deal:** a Hormuz reopening lowers gas and oil, inflation peaks, and the ECB could skip December. Wage settlements stay moderate.
- **Stalemate:** prices stay near $95-105, and the ECB hikes to 2.75% in December on 17 December. This is the market's central case.
- **Escalation:** strikes resume or shipping is hit, Brent rises towards $120, and further hikes follow. Combined with an AI sell-off and French fiscal stress, the periphery would suffer first.

Three dates give the earliest signals: US inflation on 10 November, the Abu Dhabi talks, and the Commission's autumn forecast expected mid-November.

## Explained

### The BTP-Bund spread
It is the gap between the yield on Italy's 10-year bond (BTP) and Germany's equivalent (Bund). Germany is the euro area's benchmark safe borrower, so the gap measures extra risk. Investors compare yields. When they fear for Italy, they demand a higher yield, and the spread widens. It is quoted in basis points, where 100 basis points is one percentage point. Think of it as the extra interest a bank charges a riskier borrower. This week it was 113.83 on 3 November, against 131 on 1 October and about 88-89 in September. **Key number: 113 basis points.**

### The ECB deposit rate and second-round effects
The ECB's deposit rate is the interest banks earn on cash parked at the central bank. It anchors what banks charge on loans and mortgages. Raising it slows borrowing and demand. The ECB worries about second-round effects: energy price jumps spreading into wages and prices elsewhere. It is like a fire: the first blaze is oil and gas, while the second is people demanding higher wages. Core inflation (2.5%) is the test. **Key number: 2.50%, with 2.75% possible on 17 December.**

### The national escape clause
EU fiscal rules limit deficits to 3% of GDP. A country over the line can be in an excessive deficit procedure (EDP). The escape clause lets governments temporarily deviate from spending paths for specific reasons, such as defence. Rome has asked to use it for defence and energy, and finance ministers decide around 9 November. It works like a household negotiating a one-off exemption for a big repair. **Key number: €29bn deviation across 2027-28.**

### The VIX and a leverage unwind
The VIX gauges expected volatility in US stocks over 30 days, derived from options prices. A rise signals fear. A leverage unwind is when investors who borrowed to buy assets are forced to sell as prices fall, which deepens the fall. Some analysts use that to explain this week's drop, which is a view, not a fact. **Key number: VIX 25.64, up 32% on the week.**
